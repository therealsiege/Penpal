# The Changing Center of the Patient Experience with Tristan Markwell

**Date:** January 2, 2026  
**Author:** Bobby Guelich, CEO, Elion

---

**Name:** Tristan Markwell

**Role:** Futurist/Strategist

_Tristan Markwell is a strategic foresight professional with nearly two decades of experience in healthcare, whose work lies at the intersection of data, AI, and strategy. He recently served as Data Strategy Fellow at Providence Health._

**What trends in healthcare have you been paying particular attention to?** What I’ve seen over the past 10 years is a battle for the center of the healthcare experience. Traditionally, healthcare’s center—the main relationship—has been between patients and their doctors or hospitals. That’s where care happened. Over the last decade, a lot of players have tried to take that position.

We’ve seen everything from pharmacies and retailers (e.g., Best Buy) to tech companies like Apple and Google trying to redefine where people experience healthcare. Epic’s MyChart Central is another good example. It shifts the focus from “I’m using X Health System’s MyChart to talk to my doctor” to “I’m using Epic’s MyChart to manage my health.” The platform itself becomes the center of the experience, not the health system.

**How are new care models influencing this dynamic?** There’s been a big rise in “drug-forward” models—companies like Hims & Hers or Ro that let people start with a treatment instead of a formal diagnosis. Individuals already have a sense of what’s wrong and what they want, and so seek it directly.  That’s a fundamental shift from the traditional physician-led approach.

Then there’s the “drug-plus” model. Omada, for example, pairs GLP-1s with counseling to help people maintain weight loss. There are emerging models for behavioral health where medication is paired with AI that can provide regular nudges. These models turn care into an always-on experience, rather than something episodic or reactive.

Much of this is happening outside traditional systems. It’s reshaping expectations around what care looks like and how often people engage with it.

**What does that mean for health systems?** It means the patient relationship we’ve long taken for granted is no longer guaranteed. If you ask people today, “Who’s your partner in your health?” many won’t name a specific health system.

In the ‘90s, insurers tried to take that role (i.e., the HMO era)—deciding what care people didn’t need. Today’s competition is about engagement. Some patients want to think about their health as little as possible. Others want to think about it all the time.

Health systems have to meet both groups where they are. We can’t assume people will stay loyal to the traditional model. The future belongs to organizations that design experiences for both and still deliver meaningful value in both spaces.
