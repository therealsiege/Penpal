**Name:** Paul Lukac

**Role:** Chief AI Officer

**Organization:** [UCLA Health](https://www.uclahealth.org/)

**Can you share a quick overview of your role at UCLA Health?**

I’m the inaugural Chief AI Officer at UCLA Health. I’m a pediatrician by training and also an informaticist. In my current role, I set the AI strategy in alignment with the health system’s strategy, and I chair our Health AI Council, which governs clinical and operational models to ensure we practice responsible AI.

**You mentioned part of your role involves developing UCLA Health’s AI strategy. What does that mean in practice?**

I think of it in two parts:

- Outside of my direct team, I talk about it in terms of outcomes: keep patients healthier, reduce administrative burden for clinicians, and ensure financial sustainability with real ROI—productivity gains, new revenue, or lower costs.

- Internal to my team, it’s about controlled execution: pick the right technologies, vet and test them, manage known and unknown risks, support users, and monitor performance.

Finally, to ensure our AI strategy is supporting the health system’s objectives, I need a high-level view of each model’s outcomes and ROI over time so we can validate they’re having the desired impact.

**With AI everywhere (and lots of noise), how do you decide where to focus?**

There are areas of need that become apparent just from listening to our internal teams. Like many health systems, patient access and our call center have been pain points for years, so we’re evaluating voice AI agents to augment staff, especially after hours and on weekends.

I also watch industry trends. Right now I think low-risk, high-margin areas like revenue cycle and AI-enabled RPA are a growing focus. Payers are tooling up, and we can’t fall behind in making the uncomfortable parts of medicine more efficient.

**More broadly, how do you think about your general technology strategy with respect to leveraging Epic, acquiring point solutions, or building internally?**

I’m platform-oriented. Platforms like Epic may not have the best AI feature today, but they’re durable partners, will keep investing and improving, and will be here in five years. If a platform solution is reasonable, we’ll likely use it. That said, for true gaps that are not on a platform roadmap—and hard to build internally—we’ll consider focused vendors with compelling offerings.

**Any topics or areas you’ve been grappling with that you think your peers should be watching?**

Individually procured AI tools (e.g., for differential diagnosis, or free ambient scribes). There’s real potential—but HIPAA-compliance, data privacy issues, and BAAs get murky fast when a clinician signs up solo. The current privacy frameworks didn’t anticipate LLMs or re-identification risk. Health systems and vendors need better contracting paths and clearer assurances; individual BAAs for solo users are the wrong instrument.

**How have you managed this?**

We warn staff about risks of using personal or non-enterprise accounts (e.g., public ChatGPT) and can surface advisories when certain URLs are accessed on our network. I don’t want to shut it all down—there’s value—but use needs to be safer, more standardized, and always with our patients’ privacy top of mind.

**You mentioned one of your responsibilities is monitoring AI solutions once they are live. This is an area that has been challenging for many health systems. What’s your approach?**

For traditional ML or predictive models that are custom built, we run the full MLOps lifecycle in Azure Databricks, including monitoring model performance. GenAI is harder. We’ve piloted four use cases and rely on structured, manual review, like validated surveys and required sample reviews (e.g., chart summaries, denials letters), and make go/no-go decisions based on those. Manual oversight doesn’t scale, though, so our data scientists are exploring using “LLM-as-judge.” There’s nuance—an LLM can’t always know what a clinician learns at the bedside—but we’re investigating it.
