# Digitizing Payer Contracts and Rethinking Patient Engagement with Aimmon Lago of Stanford Health Care

**Date:** September 5, 2025  
**Author:** Bobby Guelich, CEO, Elion

---

**Can you give us a quick, high-level overview of Stanford Health Care and your role there?** Stanford is an academic medical center serving all specialties with focus in oncology, neurology, cardiology, and orthopedic medicine. Stanford’s facilities consist of our main hospital and clinics on Stanford’s campus and a broader network around the San Francisco Bay Area including a community hospital, several medical office centers, and numerous medical clinics.

As an academic medical center, we have a three-part mission—training the physicians of the future, conducting advanced medical research, and caring for patients both locally and globally. As the Executive IT Director for Revenue Cycle and Population Health Systems I’m accountable for our systems that facilitate registration, scheduling, authorization, HIM, coding, billing, CRM, and value-based care.

**What are your key technology priorities right now?** Like many healthcare providers, Stanford is facing significant market forces including payer negotiations, increasing demand for care, and cost management. Technology is viewed as a solution to many of these challenges, however the pathways to value are not always clear. Like many organizations we see two paths to value; ground up ‘design thinking’, and a Lean IT approach. We aim to strike a balance between these.

**Can you give me an example of that continuous improvement work?** Reducing scheduling complexity is a good example where a Lean IT or continuous improvement approach can be applied. Across our subspecialties, our scheduling practices have become very complex over time; creating high variation across specialties and providers. This variation contributes in turn to cost and inhibits self-scheduling.

For example, in primary care, since our Epic go-live in 2009, we had developed close to 100 different visit types. This created unnecessary difficulties for our staff and patients, so we brought together clinical, administrative, and technology stakeholders to review, and consolidate visit types and scheduling practices. We continue to monitor our scheduling KPIs, add new technology workflows that eliminate waste, and train users on best practices.

**In terms of total redesign projects, what are you most excited about right now?** I’m most excited about data integration with our health plan partners. A lot of our administrative OPEX (some may call industry waste) goes into sharing information with health plans—things like eligibility, prior auth, claim status, and medical necessity documentation. Over the last two years we’ve taken deliberative steps to implement Epic’s Payer Platform to connect our EMR to payers directly. That means we can automate workflows that currently require staff to take information from our EMR, put it into a payer portal, wait, and then re-enter data back into Epic. These EDI standards have been around since HIPAA, but they’re only now starting to be usable at scale. By leveraging Epic’s design, we’re redesigning entire revenue cycle workflows by eliminating much of the manual work.

One of the challenges we’ve found in this process however, is that payers, like many providers, have substantial technical debt. They may have separate, and sometimes aging, systems for benefit administration, claims adjudication, utilization management, and quality. In some cases, even though we may have already provided access to all the clinical data for a patient, the health plan may still request a separate medical necessity letter for prior auth, because the data wasn’t shared among their internal silos. I believe the incentives are in place that this will eventually be worked out, but for the time being, it’s still an impediment to full value realization for both parties.

**There’s a common narrative right now about an “arms race” between payers and providers, especially with AI. Is what you’re describing more collaborative?** I don’t think an AI arms race (or ‘bots vs bots’) is an accurate description of the payer provider landscape, nor would it be productive to either party. Relationships between payers and providers are contract based. The first step to improving our industry is to digitize those contracts with technical standards; exchanging claims, for example, is generally done using an HIPAA 837 EDI standard. This isn’t easy given the incredible variety and complexity of a medical claims contract which can include complex payment rules based on the timing and sequence of tens of thousands of diagnosis and procedure codes. Despite the technical complexity, this is the necessary first step to eliminating administrative waste.

After this is complete, straightforward data exchange of clinical data, authorization requests, and claims through frameworks such as Epic’s Payer Platform will run with little manual intervention, and perhaps in near real-time. That’s the goal, real-time payer alignment based on an agreed digitized contract delivered to a provider at every step in the patient care process.

**Any other areas you’re really excited about?** Generative AI will improve providers’ ability to engage patients. Providers historically have engaged patients through expensive staffed phone calls and static email, SMS, and patient portal content. Generative AI promises to summarize key information for patients and share it with them in conversational voice or chat channels that are convenient, usable, and cost efficient. It will probably look and feel like a ‘ChatGPT of my medical record,’ that can be accessed by voice or text. This will improve the patient experience, and eliminate provider operating costs in areas like scheduling, appointment reminders, billing, and health maintenance reminders (like flu shots).

Adjacent to this concept of GenAI patient engagement, Epic has recently released tools within its CRM platform that can automate many of these workflows across multiple channels—bi-directional SMS, MyChart app messages, even unauthenticated guest bill pay. For organizations like ours that operate on a single EMR, this kind of platform solution makes a lot of sense.

**Speaking of Epic, how do you decide when to look outside the EMR?** We’re always objective in our solution design, by first clearly defining the problem to be solved, then doing our best to document costs and benefits over the short and long term of a solution.  Epic, or any EMR platform for that matter, makes a compelling ROI argument given their platform solutions frequently have low additional direct costs, the integration costs are low to zero, the UI is familiar to your users, the data is in one place for analytics, and the 24/7 tier 1/2/3 support structure is already in place.

This said, Epic isn’t always first to a market so, like a balanced portfolio, I think it’s reasonable that other solutions are not only considered but used. A provider organization won’t succeed with thousands of software applications, but having one monolithic system also creates risks. The Explore vs. Exploit framework comes to mind with this question, and I think the answer for many healthcare providers is to use both in some combination depending on the organization's financial and strategic position.
