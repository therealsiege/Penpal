**Date:** October 31, 2025  
**Author:** Bobby Guelich, CEO, Elion

**Name:** Jamey Shiels

**Role:** SVP, Consumer & Digital Experience

**Organization:** Advocate Health

**To start, can you share a quick overview of your role at Advocate?** Advocate Health (formed by Advocate Aurora in WI/IL and Atrium Health in NC/GA) is about 2-3 years old and the third-largest non-profit by revenue. I lead consumer and digital experience for the enterprise. My team’s work spans four pillars:

- **Experience design:** We use human-centered design to create a total experience; things like journey mapping, service blueprinting, persona development, and prototyping.

- **Consumer access and products:** This group takes those insights and turns them into tangible products, focusing on making it easy for consumers to find care.

- **Consumer relationship and data management:** That’s our CRM, marketing automation, consumer data platform, and personalization engine.

- **Digital strategy and operations:** We’re consolidating more than a hundred websites, managing content and search strategy, and keeping our digital infrastructure running day to day.

So we manage the core digital journey and extend out from there into broader experience and product design work.

**You previously mentioned that wellness is one of your focus areas. How are you approaching that topic?** One of the areas that we're strategically focused on as an organization is reimagining the consumer experience through wellness and the shift to care at home. It’s partly about diversifying revenue, but also about meeting people where they are upstream from traditional healthcare.

We know that wellness is a spectrum: Do you take your wellness advice from a clinician or a Kardashian? Through our research we identified high-level archetypes, including about 22% of our audience that we call “rule followers” who predominantly trust clinical guidance.

This archetypes work has led us to focus on wellness areas where clinical differentiation matters most. Our first product, now live, is a weight management product that combines access to GLP-1s with coaching and other clinical services. Sleep and cognition are next, and women’s health will follow.

The challenge, of course, is that clinical care delivery and consumer product design are very different disciplines. We’re figuring out how to bridge those worlds. The MVPs for these products are built internally using Epic capabilities, but we’re exploring partnerships to scale them into more consumer-friendly experiences.

**Do you see yourselves competing directly with companies like Hims and Hers and Ro?** Honestly, no. We can’t compete head-to-head on simplicity or speed; those companies are built with a very tight focus on one specific outcome, whereas we’re looking at patients more holistically and with more clinical rigor. We chose sleep and weight in particular because of their comorbidities, so from a design standpoint, what we’re doing is more broad.

They also spend heavily on marketing. I have a fraction of their budget. So we’re starting inside-out—engaging our existing patients first, where we already have relationships and trust—then gradually expanding outside-in.

**So is the wellness line meant to be profitable on its own, or more of an entry point to the broader system?** Both. In the near term, the inside-out strategy is about capturing more share of wallet with our existing patients. If they’re considering Hims and Hers for weight management or sleep, we want them to come to us instead.

Longer term, we’re exploring the outside-in model: cash-pay or membership-based programs. Think of it as adjacent to traditional care delivery, a way to diversify revenue and meet people earlier in their wellness journey.

**I want to transition to the customer data platform (CDP) side of things. How does that fit into all of this?** Healthcare has talked about “personalization” for years, but in most systems that just means getting your name right at the top of an email. We’re trying to build a real 360-degree view of the consumer, which supports three things:

1. **Consumer Expectations:** People manage their entire travel experience in an app. They expect the same from healthcare. We need to know whether they prefer virtual or in-person care, and why they’re seeking it.

2. **Growth:** Using data-driven personalization to guide people to the right care at the right time, whether that’s through targeted content, next-best actions, or AI-powered recommendations.

3. **Efficiency:** Reducing manual outreach and call volume through marketing automation and AI agents. If we can anticipate what a patient needs and communicate it proactively, that’s a win for both them and us.

**What do you make of the current CDP market in healthcare as it pertains to the nuances around data privacy and HIPAA?** The market is evolving but not quite mature. The big enterprise platforms—Salesforce, Adobe—have robust capabilities, but integrating them into healthcare environments is complex, especially with HIPAA and data-sharing restrictions. Most still push compliance responsibility onto us or our implementation partners.

Everything we build sits on a foundation of consent management, explicit or implicit. We want to be compliant and respectful of consumer choice while still being able to engage meaningfully. We haven’t found a healthcare-specific CDP that truly meets all our privacy and interoperability requirements, and that feels like a market gap.

And then, of course, there’s Epic. Epic’s great at what it does—EHRs—but it’s not a CDP. I wouldn’t be surprised if they’re building something in that space. When they make announcements like that, it can freeze the market for everyone else. But as we’ve all learned, you might wait years for it to fully mature.

So we’re not waiting. We’re modernizing now, building consent-driven data capabilities internally, and partnering selectively where it makes sense.
