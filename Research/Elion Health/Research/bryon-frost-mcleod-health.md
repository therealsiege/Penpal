# Talking Burnout Relief and Downtime Risks with Bryon Frost of McLeod Health

**Date:** August 8, 2025  
**Author:** Bobby Guelich, CEO, Elion

---

**Name:** Bryon Frost

**Role:** Chief Medical Informatics Officer

**Organization:** [McLeod Health](https://www.mcleodhealth.org/)

**Can you give us a quick overview of McLeod and your role?** I’m the CMIO at McLeod Health and still practice as an ER physician. McLeod is an eight-hospital system, which includes a Level II Trauma Center. My role centers on evaluating and implementing technology that improves care and efficiency, but within strict budget constraints. As a nonprofit without high margins or major benefactors, every investment must show hard-dollar ROI. It’s an exciting time with the advances in neural networks and generative AI, but we can’t just try things out for the sake of innovation.

**You mentioned generative AI. Where have those implementations made sense for McLeod?** Our most notable success so far has been deploying [Suki](https://elion.health/products/suki) for ambient documentation. By structuring the contract around patient encounters instead of a flat license fee, we’ve been able to align incentives with the vendor, and they have taken on risk. Adoption has been strong, and we’re seeing a net financial gain from improved CPT code capture—before even accounting for benefits like HCC capture or physician retention.

_Editor’s note: You can see more of Bryon’s perspectives on ambient scribe evaluation and implementation in our_ [_AI Scribe Roundtable here_](https://elion.health/resources/ai-ambient-scribes-health-system-lessons) _._

**Beyond ambient documentation, where else are you exploring AI through that ROI-focused lens?** We’re evaluating deep learning tools for structural heart detection based on EKGs and for interpreting echocardiogram images. These models are black-box but impressively accurate.

We’re also interested in agentic workflows, like using AI to help call center agents triage requests faster. We understand that some patients might not want to “talk to a bot,” but at the same time, we know that some patients are calling, waiting on hold, and never getting their question answered. So we’re being cautious. We also don’t want to lock into long vendor contracts in a rapidly changing space.

**Given the importance of hard dollar ROI, what drove the echocardiogram and EKG as key initial priority areas?** That’s a bit of a challenge. In a traditional fee-for-service model, every patient visit generates revenue. However, with population health initiatives, the goal shifts to preventing patients from needing to visit the hospital at all. In fee-for-service, you get paid for every encounter, but in population health, you're aiming to keep patients healthy outside of the hospital, which can be harder to measure financially. That said, we’re committed to improving patient care while keeping our costs in check through value-based contracts.

The costs really come from a few key places:

- The subscription costs, which are the most obvious.

- The IT overhead required for FTEs to maintain the solution, which a lot of people don't think about when they're doing their initial ROI assessment.

- The FTEs required to actually ingest and care for those patients.

- The somewhat opaque costs related to change management, including training, communication, and integration efforts across the organization.

My primary concern as we evaluate vendors is ensuring our operational readiness and focusing on the downstream implementation of any solution, making sure it integrates smoothly into our existing workflows and is sustainable in the long term.

**You also mentioned reducing physician burnout as a potential means of uncovering ROI. Can you say more about the role of the AI tools in reducing burnout?** I believe that note length is a very good proxy for physician burnout. I trained exclusively on paper charts, and watched note bloat and burnout grow with the 1995/1997 E/M coding guidelines. When the HITECH Act accelerated EMR adoption in 2009, documentation burdens exploded.

But now we’re finally seeing some relief: CMS retired the old guidelines in 2021 (outpatient) and 2023 (inpatient), and generative AI is helping reduce documentation overhead. That combination gives me hope.

**How do you weigh the upside of reduced note burden against reliance on technology?** The more we automate and increase cognitive offloading, ​​the more fragile the system becomes both at a human level and at an IT infrastructure level. Today’s clinicians never used paper charts or prescription pads. If systems go down, either at our local level or throughout the state or nationally, we're going to be in trouble. We’ve spent the last three months focused on downtime planning for exactly that reason.

But there’s subtlety here, too. For example, we’ve started using [Care.ai](https://elion.health/products/care-ai-virtual-nursing) for virtual nursing and are exploring applications in the OR to monitor patient flow, blood loss, instrument usage, and more. That could potentially help us optimize surgical workflows and reduce our need for adding additional surgical techs. But it’s risky if those skills atrophy and we ever need to revert to traditional methods.

**Given some of the bigger picture concerns and opportunities around AI that we’ve discussed, I’m curious to hear how you’re thinking about AI governance across the organization.** We’ve stood up an AI Governance Committee to vet all AI-related tools—clinical or operational. One of our first lessons: Even IT leaders often don’t really understand what “AI” means or the differences between machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, or basic rules-based automation. So we’re focused on education and have created a rubric to classify tools by their technical approach and associated risk. We’re also assessing ownership of data, legal risk, and alignment with our infrastructure.
