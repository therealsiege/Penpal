# Behind the Scenes of In-House and Co-Development Efforts at Mount Sinai Health System with Robbie Freeman

**Date:** August 22, 2025  
**Author:** Bobby Guelich, CEO, Elion

---

**Name:** [Robbie Freeman](https://www.linkedin.com/in/robbie-freeman-rn/)

**Role:** Chief Digital Transformation Officer

**Organization:** Mount Sinai Health System

**Can you give us a quick overview of your role and responsibilities?** I lead several teams focused on digital experience, which includes our digital assets like the MyMountSinai patient app—now with over 2 million active users—and patient journey tools for access, navigation, and triage. We also support the care team journey, including work with ambient AI, internally developed AI products, and vendor solutions. Radiology and Epic-integrated tools are big focus areas.

New this year is the workforce journey: tools for our 50,000 employees to make daily tasks easier. That starts with a new GenAI-enabled digital front door integrated with ServiceNow, Oracle Cloud, and policy systems, designed to surface what employees need with minimal clicks and personalization by role and location.

My teams span digital, AI product, and clinical informatics. The informatics group—mainly nurses embedded in hospitals and service lines—serve as translators between clinicians and technology teams, surfacing pain points and driving continuous improvement.

**Mount Sinai has strong internal engineering capacity. How do you decide whether to build internally, buy, or partner?** We use what we call an _experience-led model_. It starts with the problem we’re trying to solve; anyone in the organization can submit an idea for an AI product. We check alignment to organizational priorities and value (financial, safety, quality, experience), then ask: Do we already have the tools and technology available today?

If Epic or a current vendor can deliver, we start there, testing performance and accuracy first. If there’s a gap, we decide whether to build, buy, or partner. We’re fortunate that our internal team can build solutions when nothing on the market fits. For example, we developed an AI product to find patients in the hospital who are malnourished. That’s been a very repeatable model for us, giving us about 30% internal builds, another 20-30% that’s vended solutions, and the rest is  EMR-native. We also partner with our researchers to turn prototypes into production software.

**Is commercialization potential part of your decision-making?** Not as a primary driver. Most builds solve urgent health system problems, but some are later licensed so other organizations and patients can benefit. Co-development with vendors is appealing, especially with earlier-stage companies, and is an area where our partnerships team is active.

**How do you find co-development partners?** If there’s a gap, we scan the market; sometimes there’s no clear leader. That’s a situation that’s ideal for partnership. An example is that we had this concept of helping patients navigate their care with a symptom checker. We looked at the players in the market and nothing really met our needs. So we ended up partnering with a startup called [Clearstep](https://elion.health/products/clearstep). They were able to take our vision, based on wireframes we’d mapped out from focus groups, and build it.

**You’ve emphasized “experience-led” development. What does that look like in practice?** Our _Experience Lab_, led by [Eric Kim](https://www.linkedin.com/in/erichkim/), is all about building up the competencies to have really good design research and customer focus. Our “customers” can be patients and families, care teams, or our workforce more broadly. That team of user researchers and designers spend a lot of upfront time making sure that there's very little distance between the problem we're solving for, the solution we're building, and then executing it. This lab then works hand-in-hand with our _Digital Studio_, led by [Paul Francoviglia](https://www.linkedin.com/in/paul-francaviglia-b988a34/), which houses developers, MyChart analysts, and integration specialists to execute builds or partner implementations.

**How do you measure success in these projects?** We define success metrics before any implementation. This aligns with our agile operating model—each journey has cross-functional pods with quarterly key results mapped to system-level OKRs. For example, the contact center pod’s current KR is to increase call deflections, and all work ladders up to that.

**Can you share one high-impact initiative and maybe one that didn’t meet expectations?** A standout success is _My Next Steps_, a digital checklist for patients after visits. It guides them through actions like medication fills, specialist visits, or surgery prep. In surgical cases, it’s reduced same-day cancellations from avoidable errors like eating beforehand, improving experience and efficiency.

On the other hand, our first attempt at building an internal product for infusion chair optimization faced legacy platform limitations. We aimed to predict patient arrivals and better match supply and demand, but adoption lagged. We’re revisiting it with more off-the-shelf tech.
