**Name:** [Taylor Rhoades](https://www.linkedin.com/in/tprhoades/)

**Role:** Program Director, Responsible AI

**Organization:** [Mercy](https://www.mercy.net/)

**Can you start with a quick overview of your role?**

I serve as Mercy’s Responsible AI Program Director. Mercy is a large, nonprofit, faith-based health system operating acute care and specialty care hospitals in Missouri, Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Kansas, with $10 billion in net revenue.

In my role, I oversee how we govern, evaluate, and adopt AI across the health system to be sure AI use is aligned not only with Mercy’s mission—treating every individual with human dignity and great patient care—but also with our strategic initiatives.

**Within the responsible AI program, how do you think about what success looks like?**

For Mercy, responsible AI means AI that is safe, transparent, equitable, and accountable. We’ve built a set of ethical principles that are human-centric, bias-free, and patient-centered.

Success, for us, is twofold. First, it’s about safeguarding our patients, caregivers, and communities from risks like bias, misuse, or poor-quality models. But it’s also about enablement; building the guardrails and infrastructure that allow our teams to responsibly innovate with AI. The goal isn’t to slow things down or be a barrier. Success is when we create the structures that both protect and empower our team to leverage AI effectively.

**You’ve recently introduced the concept of AI domain stewards at Mercy. Can you explain what that is?**

The AI domain steward model grew out of our existing data governance framework. Domain stewards serve as custodians or subject matter experts within their areas: clinical, HR, finance, consumer, and others. They work with me and our AI architect to review use cases, validate risks, and connect governance back to the realities of day-to-day operations.

You could call it a federated model. It helps us scale governance across a large system like Mercy while keeping decisions close to where the impact actually happens. And it reinforces that responsible AI is a shared responsibility: Domain stewards, myself, our chief data and AI officer, and leaders across the organization all play a role.

**Where have you seen the greatest impact from AI governance so far?**

The biggest impact has been in vendor evaluation and contracting. Before our current governance processes were in place, AI features were sometimes adopted in a decentralized way. Our new governance model now ensures greater visibility, consistency, and alignment with Mercy’s mission. Now, every vendor solution goes through risk and governance review. That’s helped us:

- Protect against compliance gaps and unnecessary risks.

- Negotiate stronger AI terms in contracts, including expectations around monitoring and auditing after deployment.

- Build executive confidence that Mercy is adopting AI responsibly.

On that second point, some might say, “How much leverage do you really have when negotiating for one of these novel technologies?” While AI tools may seem novel, there are often multiple options available. This gives us the ability to make thoughtful choices and negotiate vendor terms that ensure patient safety, compliance, and long-term value.

It’s also forced us to ask the right first question: What problem are we trying to solve? Because sometimes the answer isn’t a new AI tool, but rather just automation, or better process mapping, or better workflow. And even if it is an AI tool, we can consider something we already have, EMR-based solutions, or bringing in a new vendor.

**What’s been most challenging about implementing AI governance?**

The hardest part is balancing speed and safety. Like all health systems, we feel the urgency to find solutions quickly, especially in a challenging financial environment. But experience shows that careful, deliberate adoption leads to stronger outcomes, better caregiver trust, and more sustainable value. That’s why governance is so important.

The other major challenge is AI literacy. Many leaders come from non-technical backgrounds, so part of our work is helping them understand what’s under the hood: What machine learning is, how predictive models differ from automation, and what their responsibilities are if an AI tool misbehaves. We’re working with HR to build training that sets clear expectations for caregivers and frontline leaders around responsible use and reporting.

**If you could share one lesson for other systems earlier in their AI governance journey, what would it be?**

Start small and simple. Pick one high-impact process, like vendor review or use case intake, and get that right first. Show that governance adds value by helping to solve problems, not just slowing things down.

From there, build maturity step by step. Over time you can layer in policies, risk registries, domain stewards, AI literacy programs, and community engagement. But in the beginning, focus on small wins that demonstrate value, build trust, and secure leader buy-in.
