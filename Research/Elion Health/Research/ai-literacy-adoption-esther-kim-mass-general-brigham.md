# Teaching AI Literacy and Improving Adoption with Esther Kim of Mass General Brigham

**Date:** November 20, 2025  
**Author:** Bobby Guelich, CEO, Elion

---

_This is part of our executive insights series where Elion CEO Bobby Guelich speaks with healthcare leaders about their tech priorities and learnings. For more, become a member and sign up for our_ [_email list_](https://www.elion.health/#signup) _._

**Name:** Esther Kim

**Role:** Head of Emerging Technologies

**Organization:** Mass General Brigham

**Can you start by describing your role at Mass General Brigham?** I lead the Emerging Technologies team within Mass General Brigham Digital. Our job is to evaluate new technologies and determine how they can help address the system’s strategic needs. Today, about 80 percent of our work centers on AI, but we’re also looking ahead to what we call “AI plus”: extended reality, robotics, and eventually quantum computing.

Across all of these areas, the goal is the same: enhancing patient care and supporting operational efficiency while ensuring the technologies are safe, equitable and deployed responsibly. What’s unique about our approach is that we don’t treat AI as a separate domain. Some health systems have set up AI centers of excellence or appointed chief AI officers; instead our Digital group has instead chosen to embed it in the broader ecosystem and establish a collective of subject matter experts to guide responsible, systemwide adoption.

**What are your current priorities in the emerging technology space?** Our top priorities all tie back to improving the quality of care, increasing access, and reducing workforce burden across all of our employees, not just clinicians, and supporting overall efficiency. Within that framework, AI plays a major role.

We take a “carrot, not stick” approach to adoption, offering access to approved, secure MGB tools rather than restricting use. We’ve also invested heavily in AI literacy. Just a few weeks ago, we held a 350-person “prompt-a-thon” to teach prompt engineering basics and responsible use. And we continue to refine our governance processes through an updated AI Steering Committee that oversees enterprise-wide, high-impact use cases while also reducing roadblocks so we can innovate quickly while keeping safety, ethics, and transparency in mind.

**Can you share an example of an initiative that illustrates this approach?** One great example is clinical summarization. There’s such a need to reduce administrative burden, especially as more patients arrive with hundreds of pages of outside documentation. We’re evaluating several options: native functionality within the electronic health record, third-party vendors, and internally developed agents. One internal pilot focuses on breast surgery intake. In research testing, we estimate it could save nurse practitioners and physician assistants about 25 hours per month by curating and summarizing information before the patient sees a surgeon.

We also recently rolled out AI Zone, an internally built, private LLM platform that’s now live for about 1,000 employees. It lets users safely upload patient PDFs to generate summaries, with plans to expand access to over 70,000 employees. Within AI Zone, we’re building a prompt library with templates for tasks like health communication, code generation, and translation into our eight most common patient languages.

**How do you think about validation and comfort level for these higher-risk use cases?** That’s a key challenge. The breast surgery intake project, for example, is still in research because of its complexity. We start with simpler use cases, validate performance, and then layer in more sophisticated workflows. Across the industry, continuous monitoring remains a pain point, both for internally developed and third-party models. We’ve seen performance drift and occasional hallucinations, which in clinical contexts raises the risk level significantly.

We’re evaluating vendors that specialize in AI model monitoring, though few have deep healthcare experience. Over the next few months, we’ll be running proof-of-concept projects on both the machine learning and generative AI sides to understand how well these tools perform in a healthcare setting. That’s also why we’re pacing our rollout carefully; we want to move quickly, but not safely and securely.

**Switching gears away from governance for a moment, how do you approach the build vs. buy vs. partner decision?** We’ve developed a structured process for that. When a business partner brings us a startup they’re excited about, we always start by asking: _What problem are you trying to solve?_ Then we look at what’s available from our existing vendor partners and what’s on their roadmap.

That said, startups offer agility, and we value co-development opportunities, especially when we can pair them with our researchers. For example, one of our teams developed a predictive algorithm for ear infections. It worked well in a clinical workflow, and now we’re exploring partnerships with external companies to help scale and commercialize it. That’s a win for the company, for our health system, and for the researchers who get to see their work implemented at scale.

**With so many ideas in a system like yours, how do you decide what to prioritize?** Everything starts with alignment to strategic priorities. We ask whether a proposal advances patient access, quality, or efficiency, and if it doesn’t, it goes to the back burner. For ideas that come in “bottom-up,” from our clinicians, researchers and innovators, we ensure that there’s leadership support, and that there will be funding for scalability and sustainability. When we’re looking at a “top-down” application of technology, we like to look at what we call the “buzz factor.” Is this something people are genuinely excited to use? Because adoption depends on enthusiasm and change management. And, if we took it away, would the users fight to keep it? That’s often a critical indicator of real value.

**How do you think about ROI as AI deployments scale?** We’re still refining how to measure hard ROI, especially for generative AI. With ambient documentation, for example, it’s difficult to quantify beyond physician well-being. Efficiency gains are often the most tangible measure, like reducing manual chart review or automating summarization so staff can work at the top of their license. We’re working with vendors and internal leaders to define metrics that reflect true value, not just hypothetical time savings. It’s challenging, but essential if we want to sustain investment in these technologies.
